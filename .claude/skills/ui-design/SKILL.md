1. THE MANDATE

You are responsible for the complete visual and user experience transformation of FLAME.

FLAME is an industrial flaring and carbon accounting platform currently being developed incrementally, with industrial flaring as its primary focus.

This is not a generic sustainability SaaS.

This is not a generic carbon accounting dashboard.

This is not a marketplace.

FLAME must develop a distinctive visual identity rooted in industrial infrastructure, combustion, environmental intelligence, scientific measurement and operational accountability.

The objective is to produce a product that demonstrates the visual sophistication of exceptional award-recognised digital experiences while maintaining the usability, accuracy and technical discipline expected of enterprise software.

Your responsibilities include:

- Creative direction.
- Brand visual identity.
- Typography.
- Colour system.
- Marketing website design.
- Product UI/UX.
- Information architecture.
- Data visualisation.
- Interaction design.
- Responsive design.
- Accessibility.
- Frontend implementation.
- Visual quality assurance.

You must apply this skill to all existing and future frontend work.

The result must feel intentionally designed, not assembled from a collection of AI-generated components.

2. PRODUCT IDENTITY

2.1 Understanding FLAME

The name FLAME represents an opportunity to build a recognisable identity around:

- Industrial combustion.
- Gas flaring.
- Energy infrastructure.
- Emissions.
- Environmental impact.
- Measurement.
- Monitoring.
- Scientific accuracy.
- Environmental accountability.

Use these associations to inspire the visual identity.

Do not interpret the name literally.

Do not decorate every screen with flames, fire illustrations, orange gradients or combustion animations.

The visual identity should be subtle, intelligent and recognisable.

2.2 Current product scope

FLAME is currently focused on flaring and associated carbon accounting.

Inspect the existing implementation to establish the precise capabilities available.

Do not invent features.

Do not design an operational carbon-credit marketplace.

Do not introduce credit trading, credit issuance, credit retirement or marketplace interfaces as though they currently exist.

Future capabilities must not be presented as existing functionality.

The product is being built incrementally. All design and architectural decisions must respect the current development stage.

PRECISE INDUSTRIAL ELEGANCE

This is the governing design philosophy.

FLAME should combine:

1. The visual character of premium industrial technology.
2. The sophistication of editorial digital design.
3. The precision of scientific instrumentation.
4. The clarity of enterprise software.
5. The visual storytelling of exceptional creative studios.

The desired personality:

- Confident.
- Precise.
- Restrained.
- Sophisticated.
- Technical.
- Distinctive.
- Contemporary.
- Credible.

Avoid making the product look playful, generic, overly futuristic or unnecessarily decorative.

Design decisions must communicate either brand identity, product functionality, information hierarchy or user feedback.

4. NEW OFFICIAL BRAND COLOUR PALETTE

The previous colour palette is to be replaced.

The following combination is the new proposed FLAME design direction.

Do not retain the old palette merely because its colours already exist in CSS, Tailwind configuration or component styles.

Audit and replace the old colour system throughout the complete product.

4.1 Core brand colours

BASALT — #171D1C

Role:

- Primary dark brand colour.
- Main dark backgrounds.
- Sidebar backgrounds.
- Navigation surfaces.
- Dark editorial sections.
- Primary dark typography where appropriate.

Character:
Deep, almost black industrial graphite with a subtle green undertone.

LIMESTONE — #F2EFE8

Role:- Primary light background.
- Main application canvas.
- Marketing website light sections.
- Editorial surfaces.

Character:
Warm mineral off-white, deliberately different from sterile pure white.

OXIDE — #A94732

Role:

- Signature FLAME brand accent.
- Primary action emphasis.
- Important links and selected elements.
- Distinctive brand moments.
- Selected visual highlights.

Character:
Deep oxidised red, inspired by weathered industrial metal and controlled combustion.

This is the primary signature accent.

Do not apply it indiscriminately.

PATINA — #45696B

Role:

- Secondary brand accent.
- Supporting interface elements.
- Selected charts.
- Secondary actions.
- Technical information groupings.
- Selected section backgrounds.

Character:
Muted mineral teal inspired by aged copper and industrial surfaces.

This must not turn FLAME into another generic green sustainability platform.

FLARE AMBER — #D5A84F

Role:

- Restrained highlights.
- Important numerical emphasis.
- Selected environmental indicators.
- Visual details.
- Brand accents.

Character:
Muted industrial amber.

Use dark text on amber backgrounds.

Do not use it as a universal warning colour.

4.2 Supporting colours

Define the following additional tokens:

- Deep surface: #222B28
- Elevated dark surface: #2A3531
- Muted light text: #B8C5BE
- Secondary text on light surfaces: #68736E
- Soft mineral surface: #E8E4DA
- Light border: #D5D2C8
- Dark border: #39443F
- Pure white: #FFFFFF
- Deep ink: #111715

These colours support the primary identity. They must not compete with it.

4.3 Palette distribution

Use the following as an initial visual distribution guide:

- Limestone: approximately 55%.
- Basalt: approximately 25%.
- Patina: approximately 10%.
- Oxide: approximately 7%.
- Flare Amber: approximately 3%.

These are directional proportions, not strict component-level constraints.

The purpose is to prevent overuse of accent colours.

The interface must not become a collection of brightly coloured components.

4.4 Semantic colour system

Define separate semantic colours for:

- Success.
- Warning.
- Error.
- Information.
- Pending.
- Neutral.
- Disabled.
- Critical.

Do not use the brand accent colour as a substitute for every semantic state.

Status colours must have accompanying text, icons or other visual indicators.

For example, a critical emissions-data warning should not be communicated using colour alone.

4.5 Light and dark surfaces

The application should use a sophisticated combination of light and dark surfaces.

Preferred application direction:

- Main canvas: Limestone.
- Primary text: Basalt.
- Sidebar: Basalt.
- Sidebar active state: restrained Oxide treatment.
- Supporting surfaces: soft mineral.
- Primary action: Oxide.
- Secondary action: Patina or neutral.
- Selected highlights: Flare Amber.

Dark sections should be used intentionally.

Do not convert every application screen to dark mode.

The marketing website can use larger dark editorial compositions, full-bleed imagery and contrasting light sections.

4.6 Colour accessibility

All final combinations must be validated.

Requirements:

- Normal text: minimum WCAG AA contrast of 4.5:1.
- Large text: minimum 3:1.
- Meaningful interface components and graphical objects: minimum 3:1.
- Focus indicators must be clearly visible.
- Colour must not be the sole method of communicating meaning.

Test actual combinations rather than assuming that the palette is automatically accessible.

5. TYPOGRAPHY SYSTEM

Use a deliberate three-part typographic identity.

5.1 Display font: Instrument Serif

Primary purpose:

- Marketing headlines.
- Editorial headings.
- Large brand statements.
- Selected section titles.
- High-impact visual moments.

Use it to create elegance and contrast.

Do not use it for every heading.

5.2 Interface font: IBM Plex Sans

Primary purpose:

- Application navigation.
- Body text.
- Forms.
- Tables.
- Buttons.
- Supporting information.
- Dashboard headings.
- Interface components.

It should communicate engineering discipline and readability.
5.3 Technical font: IBM Plex Mono

Primary purpose:

- Emissions values.
- Measurement units.
- Technical metadata.
- Identifiers.
- Timestamps.
- Numerical comparisons.
- Selected data labels.

Use it to reinforce technical precision.

Do not overuse monospace typography.

5.4 Typography rules

Define a complete responsive type scale.

Pay attention to:

- Font weight.
- Line height.
- Letter spacing.
- Text width.
- Paragraph rhythm.
- Heading hierarchy.
- Numerical alignment.

Do not use excessively large text in operational interfaces.

The marketing website can use expressive typography, but the application must prioritise information readability.

6. LAYOUT AND COMPOSITION

Develop a coherent layout system.

Use:

- Intentional whitespace.
- Asymmetric composition.
- Editorial grids.
- Clear alignment.
- Deliberate proportions.
- Controlled visual density.
- Strong section hierarchy.

Do not make every section a card.

Do not make every page look like a dashboard template.

Do not repeat identical three-column arrangements.

Use different composition techniques depending on the content.

Possible patterns include:

- Split layouts.
- Full-width editorial sections.
- Asymmetric grids.
- Data-dense layouts.
- Full-bleed imagery.
- Structured tables.
- Layered content.
- Minimal information panels.

The design should feel consistent without becoming repetitive.

Make important operational information immediately understandable.

Use actual available data.

Where implemented, consider:

- Total emissions.
- Reporting-period emissions.
- Scope breakdown.
- Historical trends.
- Facility comparisons.
- Flare activity.
- Data completeness.
- Outstanding submissions.
- Recent activity.
- Data-quality warnings.

Do not fabricate metrics.

Do not turn every metric into a large card.

10.2 Flaring interfaces

Study the actual data model before deciding what to display.

Where supported, represent:

- Flare identifiers.
- Facilities.
- Reporting periods.
- Flared gas volume.
- Gas composition.
- Activity measurements.
- Combustion efficiency.
- Emission factors.
- Methane emissions.
- Carbon dioxide emissions.
- CO2-equivalent results.
- Calculation methodology.
- Data sources.
- Validation status.
- Historical records.

Preserve the actual scientific and calculation methodology.

Distinguish measured, estimated and calculated values wherever supported.

Never sacrifice accuracy for visual simplicity.

10.3 Data tables

Prioritise:

- Readability.
- Numerical alignment.
- Sorting.
- Filtering.
- Search.
- Pagination.
- Clear status.
- Appropriate density.
- Responsive behaviour.

Use appropriate table structures rather than replacing everything with cards.

10.4 Forms

Use logical field grouping.

Provide clear validation, units, input guidance and feedback.

Ensure users understand the relationship between their inputs and calculated outputs.

11. DATA VISUALISATION

Choose chart types according to the information being communicated.

Use:

- Line charts for trends.
- Bar charts for comparisons.
- Stacked charts for meaningful breakdowns.
- Tables for precise values.
- Maps only where actual geographic information supports them.

Use a disciplined chart palette derived from the brand colours.

Do not use all brand colours in every chart.

Use separate, consistent colours for different data series.

Do not use the same colour to represent unrelated variables in the same visualisation.

Provide labels, units, legends, tooltips and accessible alternatives.

Never fabricate data.

12. MOTION AND INTERACTIONS

The marketing website may use sophisticated, purposeful motion.

Consider:

- Scroll-based storytelling.
- Carefully choreographed entrances.
- Image transitions.
- Section reveals.
- Subtle parallax.
- Product demonstrations.
- Navigation transitions.

The application should prioritise responsiveness and clarity.

Use:

- Immediate feedback.
- Smooth but restrained transitions.
- Clear loading states.
- Form feedback.
- Table interactions.
- Useful chart interactions.
- Appropriate modal transitions.

Do not use animation for decoration alone.

Respect reduced-motion preferences.
